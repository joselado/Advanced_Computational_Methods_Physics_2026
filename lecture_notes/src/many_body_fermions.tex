\subsection*{Contents and goals}
In Session 1 we built the Hubbard model from second quantization and solved it at the mean-field
level, and at the end we saw that mean-field theory can fail badly, even for the smallest example,
which is the Hubbard dimer at half filling. In Session 2 we started from this failure and solved
the resulting Heisenberg spin model exactly, with no restriction to product states, by
diagonalizing it directly in the spin basis. In this session we go back one more step and
diagonalize the \emph{original} fermionic Hamiltonian exactly, in the fermionic Fock space, without
first mapping it to spins. This is more than a change of language, because it allows us to see
things that a spin model cannot describe. These are the charge physics, a real chemical potential,
a real difference between an insulator and a metal, and an exact numerical test of the mean-field
prediction for the magnetization in Session 1. We will find that mean-field theory gives a
magnetization of the Hubbard chain that is wrong qualitatively, and not only quantitatively, and
this kind of failure is the reason why we want to solve problems exactly when the cost allows it.
At the end we will see how spin and charge decouple from each other as the interaction grows,
which is a many-body effect called spin-charge separation. In Session 5 we will take this exact
fermionic approach again and use tensor networks to reach much larger system sizes.

\subsection{The many-body Fock space of fermions}
\subsubsection{Counting the many-body states}
Let us start by building again the many-body tools that we used for spins in Session 2, but now
directly with fermionic creation and annihilation operators. Remember from Session 1 that a
single-particle Hamiltonian on $L$ orbitals is an $L\times L$ matrix, and diagonalizing it has a
polynomial cost. As soon as we add any quartic term, which is an interaction term, this is no
longer true. We then have to diagonalize $\Ham$ in the full many-body Fock space, which is spanned
by all the ways to distribute the electrons among the $L$ orbitals, and this space grows
exponentially with $L$.

Let us count this explicitly, starting from the smallest case. We take two spinless sites, labeled
$0$ and $1$. The many-body basis has exactly four states,
\begin{equation}
  \vac, \qquad \cre{0}\vac, \qquad \cre{1}\vac, \qquad \cre{0}\cre{1}\vac,
\end{equation}
which are the empty state, the two singly-occupied states and the doubly-occupied state, so $\Ham$
is a $4\times4$ matrix in this basis. If we add a third site, the counting becomes
$1 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 8$ states, with one empty configuration, three with one electron, three with two electrons and one
fully filled, which is $2^3$. In general, for $L$ spinless orbitals the Fock space has dimension
$2^L$. Every orbital is either empty or filled, independently of the others, so we have one binary
choice per orbital. Let us compare this with the single-particle sector alone, which is spanned by
$\cre{i}\vac$ for $i=1,\dots,L$ and only has dimension $L$. Restricting to one electron is what
gives single-particle physics a polynomial cost, and removing this restriction is what gives
many-body physics an exponential cost. Panel (b) of Figure~\ref{fig:fock-basis} shows the eight
states of three orbitals.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/many_body_fermions/fock_basis.pdf}
  \caption{The local Hilbert spaces and the many-body basis built from them. (a) The states of a
    single site. There are two for a spin-$\tfrac12$, up and down, two for a spinless fermion,
    empty and occupied, and four for a spinful fermion, empty, up, down and doubly occupied, so a
    chain of $L$ sites has $2^L$, $2^L$ and $4^L$ many-body states. (b) The complete many-body
    basis of three spinless orbitals, with a ball on every occupied orbital and the states grouped
    by their particle number $N$. Across the panel there is one empty configuration, three with a
    single electron, three with two electrons and one completely filled state, so in total there
    are $1+3+3+1 = 8 = 2^3$ basis states. Every additional orbital doubles this number, because each
    orbital is either empty or filled independently of the others, and this doubling is the
    exponential growth that separates many-body from single-particle physics.}
  \label{fig:fock-basis}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Spinful fermions and the cost of the calculation}
If the fermions have spin, each site has \emph{four} local states, $\ket{0}$, $\ket{\up}$,
$\ket{\dn}$ and $\ket{\up\dn}$, as in Session 1. We can also think of a spinful chain as $2L$
spinless orbitals, where the spin is just one more orbital index, so the Fock space has dimension
$2^{2L} = 4^L$. The exponent is twice the one of the spinless case, since every site now has two
binary choices instead of one, and panel (a) of Figure~\ref{fig:fock-basis} shows this next to
the spin and the spinless fermion. Note that this is the same counting as for the spin chains of
Session 2, since a spin-$\tfrac12$ chain with $L$ sites also has a Hilbert space of dimension
$2^L$, the same as for spinless fermions. The reason for this is that there is an exact mapping
between spinless fermions and spin-$\tfrac12$ degrees of freedom, site by site, which is called
the Jordan-Wigner transformation, and we will see it in detail in Session 5. In practice, this
means two things for the cost of our calculations.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Solving an interacting \textbf{spinless} fermionic chain costs the same as solving a
    quantum \textbf{spin} chain of the same length, $\sim 2^L$.
  \item Solving an interacting \textbf{spinful} fermionic chain, such as the Hubbard model, costs
    the square of that, $\sim 4^L$, so with the same computer we can only reach about half the
    system size.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Exact diagonalization in the Fock basis}
As in Session 2, once we have $\Ham$ written as an explicit $2^L\times2^L$ matrix in this Fock
basis, or as a $4^L\times4^L$ matrix, the ground state is its lowest eigenvector. This is a set of
$2^L$ complex coefficients, or $4^L$ of them, one for each basis configuration. Any expectation
value we want, such as $\expval{n_i}$, $\expval{S_i^z}$, correlators or spectra, is then obtained
with ordinary linear algebra on this eigenvector. There is no approximation and no restriction to
product states, which is what exact diagonalization gives us, and now we do it directly in the
fermionic language.

\subsection{The exact charge-density-wave transition of spinless fermions}
\label{sec:cdwexact}
\subsubsection{The spinless chain with nearest-neighbor interaction}
Let us now use these tools for the simplest interacting model we have, which is the spinless
nearest-neighbor chain from Session 1,
\begin{equation}
  \Ham_{\mathrm{CDW}} = -t\sum_i\left(\cre{i}\ann{i+1} + \mathrm{h.c.}\right)
    + V\sum_i \left(n_i - \tfrac12\right)\left(n_{i+1} - \tfrac12\right), \qquad n_i \equiv
    \cre{i}\ann{i}.
  \label{eq:cdw3}
\end{equation}
This is the $\Ham_{\mathrm{CDW}}$ of Session 1, with $n_i \to n_i - \tfrac12$ inside the
interaction, which is only a shift for bookkeeping. If we expand it, it adds a constant and,
automatically, a chemical potential of $-V/2$ per site. This chemical potential is chosen so that
the model is exactly at half filling, without the need to add and tune a separate $\mu$ by hand.

\subsubsection{Two solvable limits}
Two limits of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cdw3} are easy to solve directly. At $V=0$ we have the free
tight-binding chain of Session 1, which has a single cosine band filled up to the Fermi points, so
it is a metal with a unique, non-degenerate ground state. At $V\to\infty$ we can neglect the
hopping, and the interaction alone is minimized by the classical alternating pattern
$1,0,1,0,\dots$, with one electron on every other site. There are exactly \emph{two} such states,
related by a shift of the pattern by one site, and they are degenerate. This is the
charge-density wave, and its defining property is that the ground state is twofold degenerate, in
contrast with the unique metallic ground state at $V=0$. Figure~\ref{fig:cdw-limits} shows the two
limits next to each other.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/many_body_fermions/cdw_limits.pdf}
  \caption{The two solvable limits of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cdw3}, where the occupation of every site is
    drawn as the filling of a cup. (a) At $V=0$ the chain is a free tight-binding metal at half
    filling, with the same average density $\tfrac12$ on every site and a unique ground state. (b)
    As $V\to\infty$ the hopping becomes irrelevant and the interaction alone selects the alternating
    $1,0,1,0,\dots$ pattern, drawn in front. If we shift this pattern by one site we obtain a second
    state with the same energy, drawn behind it as a ghost, and this twofold degeneracy is the
    defining property of the charge-density wave.}
  \label{fig:cdw-limits}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{The exact many-body spectrum}
Exact diagonalization allows us to see this on a finite chain. We diagonalize Eq.~\eqref{eq:cdw3}
at fixed $L$ and plot the low-lying eigenvalues, measured from the ground state, as a function of
$V$. At small $V$ we see a unique ground state and then a small gap to a dense set of excited
states. This gap is a finite-size effect, roughly the level spacing $\sim t/L$ of the free-fermion
band, and it becomes smaller as we go from $L=12$ to $L=14$, as we expect if it closes in the
thermodynamic limit and leaves a continuum. As $V$ grows, one excited state separates from this
continuum and goes down toward the ground state, until it becomes exactly degenerate with it as
$V\to\infty$. This is the CDW partner state. Unlike the metallic gap, this near-degeneracy does
\emph{not} close by adding more excited states below it. Instead, a large gap opens above the
ground state, which is now twofold degenerate, and this gap grows asymptotically as
$\Delta \sim V$. This linear growth has a simple interpretation. At large $V$, the cheapest way to excite the
system is to introduce a single domain wall into the alternating pattern, which is a local defect
where the two patterns of the charge order meet. Each domain wall costs an energy of order $V$
independently of the size of the system, and this is the finite gap, independent of the size of the system,
that we see in the numerics.

\subsubsection{Comparison with mean-field theory}
Let us compare this with the mean-field treatment of the same model in Session 1. There, the
self-consistent gap also opened continuously as $V$ increased and also grew linearly at large $V$,
and here exact diagonalization confirms both features quantitatively. The reason for this is that
the CDW breaks a \emph{discrete} symmetry, which is the translation by one site, a $\mathbb Z_2$
symmetry, and the breaking of a discrete symmetry of this kind is allowed even in a
one-dimensional quantum system at zero temperature. We should keep this in mind, because in
Section~\ref{sec:hubbardmag} we will see a case where mean-field theory tries to break a
\emph{continuous} symmetry, and there the result is completely different.

\subsection{Chemical potential, conserved quantum numbers, and the charging energy}
\subsubsection{Conservation of the particle number}
So far we have kept the electron number fixed at half filling. Let us now add a chemical potential
explicitly,
\begin{equation}
  \Ham(\mu) = \Ham_{\mathrm{CDW}} - \mu \sum_i n_i \equiv \Ham_{\mathrm{CDW}} - \mu N,
  \label{eq:muterm}
\end{equation}
where $N = \sum_i n_i$ is the operator of the total particle number. The important structural
property here is that $[\Ham_{\mathrm{CDW}}, N] = 0$. The hopping moves an electron from one site
to another without changing the total number, and the interaction only counts electrons, so it
also commutes with $N$. Since $\Ham_{\mathrm{CDW}}$ and $N$ commute, they have a common eigenbasis,
which means that adding the $-\mu N$ term does not change any eigenvector of
$\Ham_{\mathrm{CDW}}$. It only shifts each eigenenergy by $-\mu N$, where $N$ is the fixed
particle number of that eigenstate. As a result, every eigenvalue of $\Ham(\mu)$ is a straight line
as a function of $\mu$, with slope $-N$ given only by the particle number of that state relative
to the one of the ground state.

This has a direct and useful consequence. Different excited states have different particle
numbers, so they have different slopes, and as we sweep $\mu$ these lines cross. At a crossing,
the \emph{ground state} itself changes, meaning that below the crossing the lowest-energy state has
$N$ electrons and above it the lowest-energy state has $N\pm1$. In other words, $\mu$ is a
parameter that directly controls the electron number of the ground state, in the same way as a
gate voltage does in a real quantum dot or nanostructure.

\subsubsection{The charging energy}
Let us make this quantitative. We define $E(N)$ as the lowest eigenvalue of $\Ham_{\mathrm{CDW}}$
restricted to the $N$-particle sector. The state with $N$ particles is the ground state of
$\Ham(\mu)$ when $E(N) - \mu N$ is lower than $E(N\pm1) - \mu(N\pm1)$, and this condition can be
written as a window,
\begin{equation}
  E(N) - E(N-1) \;<\; \mu \;<\; E(N+1) - E(N).
  \label{eq:addwindow}
\end{equation}
The width of this stability window is the \textbf{charging energy},
\begin{equation}
  U_c(N) \equiv \big[E(N+1) - E(N)\big] - \big[E(N) - E(N-1)\big],
  \label{eq:charging}
\end{equation}
which is the discrete second derivative of the ground-state energy with respect to the particle
number. It measures how much harder it is to add one more electron than it was to add the previous
one, and it is the quantity that gives the flat plateaus that you see when you plot the
ground-state occupation $\expval{N}$ against $\mu$. On a plateau, $\mu$ can change without
changing $N$, because it has not yet reached the addition energy in Eq.~\eqref{eq:addwindow}. As
$V$ grows, the interaction makes it more expensive to put more charge on sites that are already
crowded, so $U_c$ grows and the plateaus in the $\expval{N}$-versus-$\mu$ curve become wider, which
is what the notebook shows when you compare a weakly and a strongly interacting chain.
Figure~\ref{fig:charging} shows the staircase for a ring of eight sites.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/many_body_fermions/charging.pdf}
  \caption{The chemical potential as a gate on the electron number. (a) A ring of eight sites on a
    gate, which is the term $-\mu N$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:muterm}, here with $N=4$ electrons, which is
    half filling. (b) The particle number of the ground state against $\mu$ for the ring at
    $V=4t$, computed exactly from Eq.~\eqref{eq:cdw3}. We use a ring because the ends of an open
    chain blur the plateaus. Every block except the last one, which continues to arbitrarily large
    $\mu$, is a plateau, meaning the window of Eq.~\eqref{eq:addwindow} in which $N$ does not
    change, and its width is the charging energy of Eq.~\eqref{eq:charging}. The widest plateau is
    at half filling, with about $3.8\,t$ against less than $3\,t$ for its neighbors, and this width
    is the charge gap of the charge-density wave.}
  \label{fig:charging}
\end{figure}

\subsection{The exact magnetization of the Hubbard chain}
\label{sec:hubbardmag}
\subsubsection{The magnetization of the exact ground state}
Now let us include spin again and repeat this exercise for the Hubbard chain,
\begin{equation}
  \Ham_{\mathrm{Hub}} = -t\sum_{i,\sigma}\left(\cre{i\sigma}\ann{i+1\sigma} + \mathrm{h.c.}\right)
    + U\sum_i \left(n_{i\up} - \tfrac12\right)\left(n_{i\dn} - \tfrac12\right),
  \label{eq:hub3}
\end{equation}
which, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cdw3}, differs from the Hubbard Hamiltonian of Session 1 only by the
same shift for bookkeeping, which is an automatic chemical potential that keeps us at half filling.

We now meet a problem. If we diagonalize $\Ham_{\mathrm{Hub}}$ exactly and compute the local
magnetization $m_i \equiv \expval{n_{i\up}} - \expval{n_{i\dn}}$ site by site, we find $m_i = 0$
\emph{identically}, on every site and for every $U$, independently of how strong the interaction
is. This is in direct contradiction with the mean-field theory of Session 1. There, for any $U>0$
on a bipartite half-filled chain, the self-consistency loop converges to a finite staggered moment,
$m_i = (-1)^i m$, with $m$ growing toward saturation as $U\to\infty$. Exact diagonalization and
mean-field theory do not only differ quantitatively here, since they disagree about whether the
ground state is magnetic at all. Figure~\ref{fig:hubbard-moments} shows the two answers next to
each other, and it also shows where the antiferromagnetism is in the exact answer, which is in the
correlations.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/many_body_fermions/hubbard_moments.pdf}
  \caption{Two answers for the half-filled Hubbard chain of Eq.~\eqref{eq:hub3}. (a) Mean-field
    theory finds a finite staggered moment $m_i=(-1)^i m$, with an arrow on every site. (b) The
    exact ground state of a chain of six sites at $U=4t$ has no moment on any site, so every
    electron is drawn without an arrow. The antiferromagnetism is instead in the correlation
    $\expval{\vb S_0\cdot\vb S_j}$ between the first site and site $j$, drawn as an arc whose
    thickness is its size, blue where it is negative and orange where it is positive, so every arc
    has the color of the arrow that panel (a) puts on site $j$. The sign alternates with distance,
    $-0.54$ for the neighbor and $+0.12$, $-0.17$, $+0.06$, $-0.11$ further away, which is the
    staggered pattern of panel (a), now carried by correlations rather than by moments.}
  \label{fig:hubbard-moments}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Lieb's theorem}
The reason for this is an exact symmetry argument. The Hamiltonian Eq.~\eqref{eq:hub3} is
invariant under any global spin rotation, so it commutes with the total spin operators,
$[\Ham_{\mathrm{Hub}}, \vb S^2] = [\Ham_{\mathrm{Hub}}, S_z^{\mathrm{tot}}] = 0$, and its
eigenstates can be labeled by a definite total spin $S$. For the repulsive Hubbard model on any
bipartite lattice at half filling there is an exact result that fixes this completely, which is
called \textbf{Lieb's theorem}. It says that the ground state is unique and has total spin
$S = \tfrac12|N_A - N_B|$, where $N_A, N_B$ are the sizes of the two sublattices. On our chain the two
sublattices have the same size, so $S=0$ exactly, which is a spin singlet, for every $U$ and every
chain length. A unique state with total spin zero that is invariant under rotations cannot have a
nonzero expectation value of $S_i^z$ on any single site. The reason is that a spin rotation would
map it to another valid ground state with the opposite sign of $m_i$, and the uniqueness of the
ground state does not allow two different ground states. Mean-field theory, by construction, only
searches among single Slater determinants, and a Slater determinant with staggered spin breaks
spin-rotation symmetry \emph{explicitly} from the first guess. So it can never describe a
rotationally invariant, entangled singlet, which is the same structural failure that we saw for
the Hubbard dimer in Session 1.

\subsubsection{Turning on an explicit symmetry-breaking field}
We can see this failure directly if we interpolate between the exact model and the mean-field
ansatz. We add an explicit staggered field,
\begin{equation}
  \Ham(h) = \Ham_{\mathrm{Hub}} + h\sum_i (-1)^i \left(n_{i\up} - n_{i\dn}\right),
  \label{eq:stagger}
\end{equation}
which is the single-particle term that mean-field theory would have generated if it had found a
self-consistent staggered solution, as in the $\HMF$ of Session 1. At $h=0$ we recover the exact
result, $m=0$. As $h$ increases from zero, $m(h)$ grows \emph{continuously} and does not jump to a
finite value. The quantum fluctuations, which are contained in the entangled singlet ground state,
act against the ordering field, and only when $h$ is large enough to overcome these fluctuations
does $m(h)$ approach the mean-field saturation value. This is the standard test for spontaneous
symmetry breaking. The true order parameter is defined as $m \equiv
\lim_{h\to0^+}\lim_{L\to\infty} m(h,L)$, and the order of the two limits is very important. At any
finite $L$, symmetry forces $m(h{=}0,L)=0$ exactly, so we must take $L\to\infty$ \emph{first}.
Even after doing that, this limit still gives zero for the one-dimensional Heisenberg
antiferromagnet, which is the model that this one reduces to at large $U$, as we will see in
Section~\ref{sec:heisrecover}. One-dimensional quantum antiferromagnets do not have true long-range
magnetic order, only quasi-long-range order with power-law spin correlations, and this is the same
gapless behavior that we will see again in Section~\ref{sec:spincharge}. This is the reason why
mean-field theory fails so badly here. It does not only miss quantitative corrections, because it
predicts an ordered phase that does not exist in one dimension.

\subsection{The Heisenberg model from the exact Hubbard dimer}
\label{sec:heisrecover}
\subsubsection{The singlet-triplet gap of the Hubbard dimer}
Let us now verify with exact diagonalization the superexchange result $J \sim t^2/U$ that we
derived in Session 1 with degenerate perturbation theory. We take the Hubbard dimer, $L=2$, at half
filling, and work in the sector with zero total $S_z$, which is spanned by the four states
\begin{equation}
  \ket{1} = \cre{0\up}\cre{1\dn}\vac, \quad
  \ket{2} = \cre{0\dn}\cre{1\up}\vac, \quad
  \ket{3} = \cre{0\up}\cre{0\dn}\vac, \quad
  \ket{4} = \cre{1\up}\cre{1\dn}\vac,
\end{equation}
which are the same singly- and doubly-occupied configurations as in Session 1. The hopping
connects a singly occupied state to a doubly occupied one, but never $\ket{1}$ directly to
$\ket{2}$ or $\ket{3}$ directly to $\ket{4}$. With the shift in Eq.~\eqref{eq:hub3}, the
singly-occupied states are at $-U/2$ and the doubly-occupied ones at $+U/2$. So if we add the
constant $U/2$ to all of them, in this basis $\Ham_{\mathrm{Hub}}$ becomes
\begin{equation}
  \Ham_{\mathrm{Hub}} \;\longrightarrow\;
  \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & -t & t \\ 0 & 0 & t & -t \\ -t & t & U & 0 \\ t & -t & 0 & U\end{pmatrix}
  \quad\text{in the basis } \{\ket1,\ket2,\ket3,\ket4\}.
\end{equation}
This block can be diagonalized easily if we use symmetric and antisymmetric combinations, which are
$\ket{a}=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket1+\ket2)$, the $S_z=0$ member of the triplet, $\ket{b}=
\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket1-\ket2)$, the singlet, and in the same way $\ket{c}=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket3+\ket4)$
and $\ket{d}=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket3-\ket4)$ for the doubly-occupied states. A short calculation
shows that $\ket{a}$ and $\ket{c}$ are already exact eigenstates,
\begin{equation}
  \Ham_{\mathrm{Hub}}\ket{a} = 0\cdot\ket{a}, \qquad \Ham_{\mathrm{Hub}}\ket{c} = U\ket{c},
\end{equation}
while $\ket{b}$ and $\ket{d}$ mix into a $2\times2$ block,
\begin{equation}
  \Ham_{\mathrm{Hub}}\big|_{\{b,d\}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2t \\ -2t & U\end{pmatrix}
  \;\Longrightarrow\;
  E_\pm = \frac{U \pm \sqrt{U^2 + 16t^2}}2.
\end{equation}
The lower eigenvalue, $E_-$, is the exact ground state. We compare it with the $S_z=\pm1$ states
$\ket{\up\up}$ and $\ket{\dn\dn}$, which are exactly degenerate and both have energy $0$, like
$\ket a$, because Pauli exclusion blocks all hopping for parallel spins. We then see that the full
ground state \emph{is} the total spin singlet ($S=0$), strictly below the $S=1$ triplet at energy
$0$, as Lieb's theorem guaranteed in Section~\ref{sec:hubbardmag}. The exact singlet-triplet gap is
therefore
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\mathrm{s-t}} = 0 - E_- = \frac{\sqrt{U^2+16t^2} - U}{2}
  \;\xrightarrow[U\gg t]{}\; \frac{4t^2}{U},
  \label{eq:exactgap}
\end{equation}
which reproduces, with a completely independent and exact calculation, not only the $t^2/U$
scaling but also the same numerical prefactor that Session 1 obtained with perturbation theory for
the effective coupling $J=4t^2/U$ of $\Ham_{\mathrm{eff}} = J\,\vb S_1\cdot\vb S_2$. If we expand
Eq.~\eqref{eq:exactgap} to leading order in $t/U$ we obtain $\Delta_{\mathrm{s-t}}\to4t^2/U$, with
exactly the same prefactor. This is a useful check, since two completely different techniques, one
perturbative and with spins only, and one exact and fermionic, give the same physics and not only
the same scaling.

\subsubsection{Excitations as magnons}
We can study the excited states further, as in Session 2, by adding a Zeeman field $-h\,S_z^{\mathrm{tot}}$. Since $[\Ham_{\mathrm{Hub}}, S_z^{\mathrm
tot}]=0$, this again leaves every eigenvector unchanged and only shifts the eigenenergies linearly
in $h$, by an amount given by the $S_z^{\mathrm{tot}}$ of each state. The three states that we
called the triplet split linearly into $S_z^{\mathrm{tot}} = -1, 0, +1$ branches, while the singlet
ground state, which has $S_z^{\mathrm{tot}}=0$, does not move at all. This is the same field
dependence as for the Heisenberg dimer of Session 2, and the physical interpretation is also the
same. The lowest excitations above a singlet ground state are spin-$1$ objects, which are called
magnons, and their Zeeman splitting is a direct and numerically exact signature of the spin physics
inside the fermionic Hubbard model. The same mapping works, qualitatively, for longer chains, since
the exact fermionic spectrum at large $U/t$ reproduces the exact spin spectrum of a Heisenberg
chain with $J_{ij}\sim t_{ij}^2/U$, and this is what it means to say that the two models are
equivalent at strong coupling.

\subsection{Spin-charge separation}
\label{sec:spincharge}
We now have all the elements to understand one of the main features of the one-dimensional Hubbard
model, which is that at strong coupling its charge and spin degrees of freedom behave in completely
different ways.

\subsubsection{The many-body gap is not one thing}
Equation~\eqref{eq:exactgap} already showed that the singlet-triplet gap of the dimer decreases like
$1/U$ at large $U$. If we follow the same gap as a function of the chain length $L$, this trend
remains and becomes even stronger. At $U=0$ the gap is a purely single-particle level spacing that
comes from the finite size, and it closes as a clean power law, $\Delta \sim 1/L$. Turning on $U$
makes the gap \emph{smaller} at fixed $L$, and increasing $U$ further makes it even smaller. At
first this looks wrong, since we expect the Hubbard model to become more insulating as $U$ grows,
not less gapped. The answer is that this gap is the \textbf{spin gap}, which is the energy needed
to change the total spin from $S=0$ to $S=1$, and in Section~\ref{sec:heisrecover} we saw that it
is given by $J\sim t^2/U$, which indeed decreases with $U$. In the thermodynamic limit this spin
gap vanishes, because the Heisenberg chain that the Hubbard model maps to at strong coupling is
itself gapless. All this does not tell us anything yet about the \textbf{charge gap}, the energy
needed to add or remove an electron, which is a completely different quantity that we have to
compute separately.

\subsubsection{Nonlocal correlators of a metal and an insulator}
A simple way to distinguish a metal from an insulator, using only the ground state, is to look at
how a static correlator decays with distance. We define the nonlocal particle correlator and the
nonlocal spin correlator,
\begin{equation}
  C_c(i,j) \equiv \expval{\cre{i\sigma}\ann{j\sigma}}, \qquad
  C_s(i,j) \equiv \expval{\vb S_i \cdot \vb S_j},
  \label{eq:corrdefs}
\end{equation}
both computed in the many-body ground state. Physically, $C_s(i,j)$ measures how strongly the spin
at site $i$ is correlated with the spin at site $j$. Even at strong coupling, where the model
reduces to the gapless Heisenberg chain, $C_s(r)$ decays as a power law and not exponentially,
because the spin sector has no gap that suppresses the propagation over long distances. What grows
with $U$ is the \emph{amplitude} of this correlator, not its decay rate. At $U=0$ the spin of each
site is only weakly correlated with its neighbors, since itinerant electrons barely feel the spin
of the other electrons. At large $U$ every site has a singly-occupied local moment that is better
and better formed and strongly antiferromagnetically correlated with its neighbors, so the
correlations approach the ones of the ideal Heisenberg chain.

$C_c(i,j)$, on the other hand, measures the amplitude to add an electron at site $i$ and later find
it at site $j$. In a metal this decays only as a power law, $C_c(r)\sim \sin(k_F r)/r$, because the
gapless charge excitations allow the particle to propagate arbitrarily far at almost no energy
cost. In an insulator, instead, any propagation needs a virtual excitation with an energy of the order of
the charge gap $\Delta_c$, and the correlator decays exponentially, $C_c(r) \sim e^{-r/\xi_c}$, with a
correlation length $\xi_c$ set by $\Delta_c$, roughly $\xi_c \sim v/\Delta_c$ for some
characteristic velocity $v$. This is the behavior we see in the Hubbard chain. At $U=0$, $C_c(r)$
decays slowly, as a power law, while at large $U$, say $U=10\,t$, it goes to zero within a few
sites, which is a clear exponential decay. This shows that the charge sector is gapped and
insulating, in agreement with the linearly growing charge scale that we will see again below. So
the interactions act in opposite directions on the two channels. They strongly suppress the charge
fluctuations while they make the spin correlations stronger, even though the energy scale
$J\sim t^2/U$ of these spin fluctuations becomes smaller.

\subsubsection{Dynamical correlators and the two energy scales}
Static correlators tell us about decay lengths, and dynamical correlators tell us about the
excitation energies, which makes them the many-body generalization of the single-particle density
of states of Session 1. We define the dynamical particle and spin correlators with their Lehmann
representations, which are spectral representations,
\begin{equation}
  S_c(\omega) = \sum_n \left|\bra{n}\cre{i\sigma}\ket{0}\right|^2 \delta\big(\omega -
  (E_n-E_0)\big), \qquad
  S_s(\omega) = \sum_n \left|\bra{n}S_i^+\ket{0}\right|^2 \delta\big(\omega - (E_n-E_0)\big),
  \label{eq:dynamical}
\end{equation}
where the sums run over all many-body eigenstates $\ket{n}$ of $\Ham_{\mathrm{Hub}}$. $S_c(\omega)$
selects the excitation energies that we reach by adding a single electron, which is the charge
sector, and $S_s(\omega)$ selects the excitation energies that we reach by flipping a single spin,
which is the spin sector. At a large but fixed interaction, say $U=60t$, the two are very
different. $S_c$ has almost no weight up to a large threshold energy, and this threshold moves up
as $U$ increases. $S_s$ has weight starting at a much lower energy, and its overall lineshape
closely follows the spin structure factor of the Heisenberg chain from Session 2, as we expect from
the mapping at strong coupling.

If we follow the peak position of each correlator as a function of $U$, the difference becomes
completely explicit. The leading charge peak moves to higher energy \emph{linearly} in $U$, since
it costs $\sim U/2$ to overcome the local repulsion when we add a second electron to a site that is
already singly occupied. The leading spin peak instead moves to \emph{lower} energy, decreasing $\sim
1/U$, as we derived in Section~\ref{sec:heisrecover}. Charge and spin excitations follow opposite
trends with the same parameter $U$, and their energies move apart without bound as $U$ increases.
This is what we call \textbf{spin-charge separation}. In the strongly correlated one-dimensional
Hubbard chain, charge transport is exponentially suppressed, and essentially absent in the
thermodynamic limit, while spin transport remains gapless and, in this precise sense, easy, even
though both live on the same lattice of electrons. Figure~\ref{fig:spin-charge} shows what the two
excitations are and how far apart their energies are.

  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/many_body_fermions/spin_charge.pdf}
  \caption{Spin-charge separation in the half-filled Hubbard chain of Eq.~\eqref{eq:hub3}.
    (a) Behind, an electron is removed from the antiferromagnetic chain, and in front, the same
    chain is shown after the hole has hopped three sites to the right. The hole is now an empty site
    without spin, which is called the holon and carries the charge. Where it started, it has left

    has become two objects that move apart. (b) The two energy scales at $U=10t$, drawn to scale as
    two towers. Adding an electron on a singly occupied site makes a doubly occupied site, called a
    doublon, which is what $S_c(\omega)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:dynamical} reaches, and it costs about

\subsection*{Closing summary}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Exact diagonalization directly in the fermionic Fock space has a Hilbert space dimension of
    $2^L$ for spinless fermions or $4^L$ for spinful fermions, which in the spinless case is the
    same as the $2^L$ cost of the equivalent spin problem of Session 2, and it has no restriction
    to product states.
  \item The spinless nearest-neighbor chain shows an exact charge-density-wave transition, in
    which a unique metallic ground state at $V=0$ becomes a twofold-degenerate, gapped insulator as
    $V\to\infty$, with a large-$V$ gap that grows as $\sim V$. This is in good qualitative and
    quantitative agreement with the mean-field treatment of Session 1, since the broken symmetry
    here is only discrete.
  \item A conserved chemical potential term allows us to control the particle number of the ground
    state directly, and the width of the resulting occupation plateaus is the charging energy,
    which is the discrete second derivative of the ground-state energy with respect to $N$.
  \item The exact magnetization of the Hubbard model is \emph{zero} at every site and for every
    $U$, in sharp and structural disagreement with the mean-field prediction of finite staggered
    order in Session 1. Lieb's theorem guarantees a unique, rotationally invariant spin singlet
    ground state at half filling, which no single Slater determinant can describe. This is the
    exact numerical confirmation of the failure of mean-field theory that we pointed out at the end
    of Session 1.
  \item An exact diagonalization of the Hubbard dimer reproduces the $t^2/U$ superexchange scaling
    of Session 1 from first principles, and adding a Zeeman field shows that the low-energy
    excitations are magnons, as for the Heisenberg dimer of Session 2.
  \item At strong coupling the Hubbard chain shows spin-charge separation. The charge sector is
    gapped, with a gap that grows linearly in $U$ and nonlocal correlators that decay
    exponentially, while the spin sector stays gapless, with a spin gap that decreases as $1/U$
    and power-law correlators, which is the physics of the Heisenberg chain it maps to.
  \item All of this was limited by the growth as $2^L$ and $4^L$, to $L\sim20$ sites for spinless
    fermions and about half that for the Hubbard chain. In Session 5 we will come back to this
    same fermionic approach, with the Jordan-Wigner mapping and tensor-network methods, and reach
    system sizes where the thermodynamic limit becomes directly visible.
\end{itemize}
