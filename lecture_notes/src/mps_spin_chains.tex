\subsection*{Contents and goals}
In the last two sessions we solved quantum many-body problems exactly. First we solved the
Heisenberg chain in the spin basis, and then the spinless and Hubbard chains in the fermionic
basis. This worked, and the gaps, the level crossings and the correlators all came out of the
numerics with no approximation. However, both times we found the same limitation. The Hilbert
space of $L$ spin-$\tfrac12$'s has dimension $2^L$, and $4^L$ for spinful fermions, and this number
grows too fast for us to handle. At around $L\sim 20$ spins even a very good computer runs out of
memory, and we are still far from the thermodynamic limit, which is where the interesting physics
appears, such as quantum phase transitions, fractionalization and the emergence of gaps. So the
question for this session is concrete. Is there a way to represent the many-body wavefunction that
does not have an exponential cost, at least for the states we are interested in? The answer is yes,
and the method is called matrix product states (MPS), which is the simplest member of the larger
family of tensor network methods. We will see why this representation is efficient, why it works
so well in one dimension, and how to check numerically if we can trust it. In the second half of
the session we will repeat the study of the Heisenberg model of Session 2, but now at $40$ to $100$
spins and more, which is large enough to see the physics of the thermodynamic limit that before we
could only guess.

\subsection*{Learning outcomes}
By the end of this session, you should be able to do the following.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Explain why the matrix product state ansatz compresses an exponentially large
    wavefunction into a cost that is linear in $L$ and quadratic in the bond dimension $\chi$.
  \item Use entanglement entropy and the area law to explain why matrix product states work well
    in one dimension, and where we expect them to fail, which is in 2D and for volume-law states.
  \item Check the convergence of a variational MPS calculation with energy fluctuations, and use
    entanglement entropy as a diagnostic for a quantum phase transition.
  \item Run MPS calculations at $40$ to $100$ spins and more to distinguish the gapless spin-$\tfrac12$
    Heisenberg chain from the gapped and fractionalized spin-$1$ Haldane phase.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{From an exponential wavefunction to a matrix product state}

\subsubsection{Tensor networks as data compression}
Before we write any equations, let us start with an analogy. Let us take a photograph with a high
resolution, and let us say that we want to store it with much less memory. We do not remove
information at random. Instead, we use the fact that real photographs have a lot of structure,
since pixels that are close to each other are correlated, and a compressed format such as JPEG can
give an image that looks the same as the original with only a small fraction of the original
pixels. Tensor networks do the same thing, but for the wavefunctions of quantum many-body systems.
The role of the pixels is played by the $2^L$ complex coefficients of the wavefunction in the
computational basis. The role of the compression algorithm is played by a specific way of writing
these coefficients as a product of small pieces, and the parameter that controls how much we
compress is what is called the \textbf{bond dimension}. In this section we will make this analogy
precise.

\subsubsection{The formal matrix product state ansatz}
For a chain of $L$ spins, each with $d$ local states where $d=2$ for spin-$\tfrac12$, $d=3$ for
spin-$1$ and so on, an arbitrary wavefunction is
\begin{equation}
  \ket{\Psi} = \sum_{s_1,\ldots,s_L} C(s_1,\ldots,s_L)\, \ket{s_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{s_L},
  \label{eq:generalwf}
\end{equation}
and to specify $\ket{\Psi}$ exactly we have to give all $d^L$ numbers $C(s_1,\ldots,s_L)$
independently. The matrix product state ansatz makes a specific proposal for how to generate these
coefficients without storing them one by one. For every site $n$ and every local state $s_n$, we
introduce a matrix $M_n^{s_n}$, and we write
\begin{equation}
  C(s_1,\ldots,s_L) = M_1^{s_1} M_2^{s_2} \cdots M_L^{s_L}.
  \label{eq:mpsansatz}
\end{equation}
For open boundary conditions, $M_1^{s_1}$ is a $1\times\chi_1$ row vector and $M_L^{s_L}$ is a
$\chi_{L-1}\times 1$ column vector, so the product of matrices in Eq.~\eqref{eq:mpsansatz} gives a
single number, as it must for $C$ to be a scalar coefficient. The internal matrices $M_n^{s_n}$ are
$\chi_{n-1}\times \chi_n$, and the integer $\chi$ is the \textbf{bond dimension}, which we will
mostly take uniform along the chain. The bond dimension is the one parameter that controls how much
this ansatz can represent, and it will be the central quantity of this session.
Figure~\ref{fig:mps-chain} shows the two ways of storing the same state next to each other.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/mps_spin_chains/mps_chain.pdf}
  \caption{The state of Eq.~\eqref{eq:generalwf} stored in two ways. (a) The coefficients
    $C(s_1,\ldots,s_L)$ as one tensor with $L$ physical legs, one per site, which means $d^L$
    numbers to store. (b) The matrix product state of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mpsansatz}, where the same
    legs hang from a chain of small tensors. Each site has one tensor $M_n^{s_n}$, with a vertical
    \emph{physical} leg that runs over the $d$ local states of that site and horizontal
    \emph{bond} legs of dimension $\chi$ that join it to its neighbors. Joining two legs means
    summing over the shared index, so contracting the horizontal legs from left to right is the
    matrix product of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mpsansatz}. The two end tensors have a single bond leg each,
    a row vector and a column vector, which is why the whole contraction gives one number. The
    only difference between the panels is where the numbers are stored, either in one block that
    grows as $d^L$ or in $L$ small tensors of $d\chi^2$ numbers each, as in
    Eq.~\eqref{eq:paramcount}.}
  \label{fig:mps-chain}
\end{figure}

Let us count parameters, because this is the main point. Storing $C(s_1,\ldots,s_L)$ directly
costs $d^L$ numbers, which is exponential in $L$. Storing the matrices $\{M_n^{s_n}\}$ instead
costs
\begin{equation}
  N_{\mathrm{MPS}} \sim L\, d\, \chi^2,
  \label{eq:paramcount}
\end{equation}
which is \emph{linear} in the system size $L$ and only quadratic in the bond dimension $\chi$. So
if we can represent the states we are interested in with a fixed and moderate $\chi$, we have
turned an exponential problem into a polynomial one. This raises a natural question. Is it true
that the states we are interested in, which are ground states and low-lying excited states of
local Hamiltonians, can be described in this way with a $\chi$ that stays moderate as $L$ grows?
We will answer this in detail once we introduce entanglement entropy in
Section~\ref{sec:entanglement}, but the short answer is yes, in one dimension, and this is the
reason why this course has a Session 4.

\subsubsection{The cost of exact diagonalization and of matrix product states}
Before we justify this theoretically, let us first look at what it gives us in practice. If we
solve the Heisenberg chain $\Ham_{\mathrm{Heis}} = J\sum_n \vb S_n\cdot\vb S_{n+1}$ by exact
diagonalization, the time to find the ground state grows exponentially with $L$, because we work
with vectors and matrices of size $2^L$. If instead we solve it variationally with the matrix
product state ansatz of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mpsansatz}, at fixed bond dimension the cost grows close to
linearly in $L$. The two curves cross at around $L\sim 14$ to $16$. Below this size exact
diagonalization is faster, since there is no reason to use a variational method when the exact
answer is cheap. Above it, matrix product states are faster, and the difference keeps growing with
the system size. For example, systems of $50$, $70$ and even $80$ spins can be solved on a laptop
in seconds, while exact diagonalization would need more memory than the largest supercomputers
have once we pass $L\sim 40$ to $50$. This difference in scaling, exponential versus polynomial,
is the basis of the rest of the session.

\subsection{Convergence diagnostics for the variational state}
\subsubsection{A variational ansatz}
Matrix product states are a variational ansatz. We do not solve the Schr\"odinger equation
exactly, and instead we search for the best approximation to the ground state, or to an excited
state, within the family of states described by Eq.~\eqref{eq:mpsansatz} at some fixed $\chi$.
This gives a practical question that we did not have with exact diagonalization. How do we know if
our variational state is good, without knowing the exact answer?

\subsubsection{Energy fluctuations}
Let us assume that our solver has converged to some normalized state $\ket{\Omega}$, with energy
$E_\Omega = \expval{\Ham}{\Omega}$. If $\ket{\Omega}$ was an exact eigenstate of $\Ham$, then
$\Ham\ket{\Omega} = E_\Omega\ket{\Omega}$ and the variance of the energy,
\begin{equation}
  (\Delta E)^2 \equiv \expval{\Ham^2}{\Omega} - \expval{\Ham}{\Omega}^2,
  \label{eq:varH}
\end{equation}
would be exactly zero. So a natural diagnostic is to compute $(\Delta E)^2$ using only the
variational state, without any knowledge of the true ground state, and to check how close it is
to zero. Let us make this quantitative. We write $\ket{\Omega}$ as mostly the true ground state
$\ket{0}$ with a small admixture $\varepsilon$ of the first excited state $\ket{1}$,
\begin{equation}
  \ket{\Omega} = \sqrt{1-\varepsilon^2}\,\ket{0} + \varepsilon\,\ket{1}, \qquad \varepsilon\ll 1.
\end{equation}
If we expand $\expval{\Ham}{\Omega}$ and $\expval{\Ham^2}{\Omega}$ to $\Order{\varepsilon^2}$
using $\Ham\ket{0}=E_0\ket{0}$ and $\Ham\ket{1}=E_1\ket{1}$, a short calculation gives
\begin{equation}
  E_\Omega - E_0 = \varepsilon^2(E_1-E_0), \qquad
  (\Delta E)^2 = \varepsilon^2(E_1-E_0)^2 + \Order{\varepsilon^4}.
  \label{eq:varHresult}
\end{equation}
Two results follow from this, and both make this diagnostic very useful. First,
$(\Delta E)^2 \to 0$ if and only if $\varepsilon\to 0$, meaning that $\ket{\Omega}$ converges to a
true eigenstate, so the fluctuation is a good measure of the unknown distance to the exact answer.
Second, if we compare the two relations in Eq.~\eqref{eq:varHresult}, we find that
$(\Delta E)^2 \approx (E_\Omega - E_0)(E_1-E_0)$, so the energy fluctuation tells us at the same
time how much our state is mixed with an excited state \emph{and} roughly how large the gap to
this excited state is. In practice we follow $(\Delta E)^2$ as we increase the bond dimension
$\chi$. It should decrease monotonically and finally become flat near machine precision once
$\chi$ is large enough for the physics of the problem.

\subsubsection{Bond dimension and excited states}
Everything we said for the ground state also applies to any targeted eigenstate. An excited-state
solver also converges toward some $\ket{\Omega_k}\to\ket{k}$ as $\chi$ increases, and we can follow
the same energy fluctuation, Eq.~\eqref{eq:varH}, evaluated on $\ket{\Omega_k}$. What changes is
\emph{how fast} this convergence happens. Higher excited states are in general more entangled than
the ground state, since they explore a larger part of the Hilbert space. As we will see in
Section~\ref{sec:entanglement}, entanglement is what a finite bond dimension has difficulty to
capture, so these states need a larger $\chi$ before their energy fluctuation goes to zero. This
is a generic feature of tensor network calculations. The ground state converges first and fastest,
and each higher excited state needs a bit more bond dimension to reach the same convergence.

\subsection{Low-energy excitations and level crossings}
Before we build the full formalism of entanglement entropy, let us see what matrix product states
give us physically, using as a diagnostic the same first excited state that we just discussed.

\subsubsection{The lowest spin excitation in the bulk}
Let us take the Heisenberg chain and compute the matrix element $\bra{0}S_n^x\ket{1}$ between the
ground state and the first excited state, as a function of the site $n$ where we act with the spin
operator. Physically, this is the amplitude for creating the lowest-energy spin excitation at site
$n$. For the spin-$\tfrac12$ chain, this profile looks like the ground-state wavefunction of a
particle in a box, with maximal amplitude at the center of the chain and a smooth decay toward
both edges. This is the particle-in-a-box picture that we already saw in the dynamical correlators
of Session 2. The lowest spin excitation is a wave spread over the whole chain, and on an
\emph{open} chain the boundaries turn it into a standing wave that vanishes at both edges. This is
the behavior of a delocalized spin excitation in the bulk, and it is our first hint that the
low-energy physics of the spin-$\tfrac12$ chain is in the bulk. Once we have systems large enough
to make the comparison convincing, in Section~\ref{sec:fractionalization}, we will come back to
this profile and compare it with the very different one of spin-$1$.

\subsubsection{Level crossings in a magnetic field}
The other natural quantity to follow is how the ground state changes as we turn on a magnetic
field, $\Ham = J\sum_n \vb S_n\cdot\vb S_{n+1} - B\sum_n S_n^z$. Since $\sum_n S_n^z$ commutes with
the exchange term, the field $B$ never mixes eigenstates of $\Ham|_{B=0}$. It only shifts their
energies by $-B\times(\text{total }S^z)$, and sectors with different total $S^z$ cross each other
as $B$ increases. For $L=2$ there is exactly one such crossing, from the $S=0$ singlet to the
$S=1$ triplet. For $L=4$ there are two, $S=0\to S=1\to S=2$, and as we increase $L$, more and more
crossings appear, closer and closer together in $B$. This is a direct and numerically clean way to
see that the spin-$\tfrac12$ Heisenberg chain is gapless in the thermodynamic limit. The staircase
of crossings becomes a smooth ramp as $L\to\infty$, meaning that there are excitations arbitrarily
close in energy to the ground state at any nonzero $B$.

\subsection{Entanglement entropy and why matrix product states work in one dimension}
\label{sec:entanglement}
For two sections we have used the matrix product state ansatz without justifying it. Let us now do
this properly, because the justification also tells us when this approach will fail.

\subsubsection{Separable versus entangled states}
Let us split our system into two regions, $A$ and $B$, where we can take $A$ as the left half
of the chain and $B$ as the right half. We say that a state $\ket{\Psi}$ is \textbf{separable} if
it can be written as a simple tensor product,
\begin{equation}
  \ket{\Psi} = \ket{\phi}_A \otimes \ket{\chi}_B,
\end{equation}
for some states $\ket{\phi}_A$ and $\ket{\chi}_B$ that live only on $A$ and only on $B$. A basis
state such as $\ket{\up\dn}$ is trivially of this form, $\ket{\up}_A\otimes\ket{\dn}_B$. A state
where every spin points along $x$ is also of this form, which is less obvious but follows
directly, since
$\ket{\to}\otimes\ket{\to} = \tfrac12(\ket{\up}+\ket{\dn})\otimes(\ket{\up}+\ket{\dn})$ is still a
product. Every classical configuration, and in fact every state that we can write without any
correlation between $A$ and $B$, is separable in this sense. But now let us take the two-site
singlet that we already saw in Session 1,
\begin{equation}
  \ket{\Psi_{\mathrm{singlet}}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Big(\ket{\up\dn} - \ket{\dn\up}\Big).
\end{equation}
No choice of $\ket{\phi}_A$ and $\ket{\chi}_B$ gives this state. If we try to write it as a
product, we find that we need two different terms with opposite relative sign, which a single
product cannot give. We call such a state \textbf{entangled}. This is the distinction between
classical and quantum states, and we need a way to quantify \emph{how} entangled a state is, and
not only whether it is entangled or not.

\subsubsection{The reduced density matrix and the von Neumann entropy}
Given the full density matrix $\rho = \ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}$, we define the \textbf{reduced density
matrix} of subsystem $A$ by tracing out everything in $B$,
\begin{equation}
  \rho_A \equiv \operatorname{Tr}_B\rho.
\end{equation}
The \textbf{entanglement entropy} across the cut is then the usual von Neumann entropy of
$\rho_A$,
\begin{equation}
  S_A \equiv -\operatorname{Tr}\big[\rho_A \ln \rho_A\big].
  \label{eq:vonNeumann}
\end{equation}
If $\ket{\Psi}$ is separable, $\rho_A = \ket{\phi}\bra{\phi}$ is a rank-one projector, its only
nonzero eigenvalue is $1$, and $S_A = -1\ln 1 = 0$. If $\ket{\Psi}$ is the singlet, a direct
computation of $\rho_A$, where we trace out site $B$, gives $\rho_A = \tfrac12\mathbb{1}$, with
two equal eigenvalues $\tfrac12,\tfrac12$, so $S_A = -2\times\tfrac12\ln\tfrac12 = \ln 2$, which
is the maximal possible entropy for a single spin-$\tfrac12$. So $S_A$ goes smoothly from no
entanglement to maximal entanglement, as we want.

\subsubsection{The Schmidt decomposition and the bound on the entanglement}
Now let us connect this to the matrix product state ansatz. We cut the chain at bond $n$, between
sites $n$ and $n+1$. We can reshape the coefficient tensor of any state as a matrix between the
left block $(1,\ldots,n)$ and the right block $(n+1,\ldots,L)$, and with a singular value
decomposition of this matrix we can write the state in the \textbf{Schmidt form}
\begin{equation}
  \ket{\Psi} = \sum_{\alpha=1}^{r} \lambda_\alpha \ket{\alpha}_A \otimes \ket{\alpha}_B,
  \qquad \sum_\alpha \lambda_\alpha^2 = 1,
  \label{eq:schmidt}
\end{equation}
with $\ket{\alpha}_A$ and $\ket{\alpha}_B$ orthonormal on each side and $r$ the Schmidt rank. For
a generic state, $r$ can be as large as $d^{\min(n,L-n)}$, which is exponential in the block size.
However, for a matrix product state built as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:mpsansatz}, the sum over the bond
index at position $n$ runs only over $\chi_n$ values by construction. This means that the
coefficient tensor factorizes through an index of dimension $\chi_n$, so the Schmidt rank at that
cut is at most $\chi_n$. If we put the Schmidt form into Eq.~\eqref{eq:vonNeumann}, we get
$\rho_A = \sum_\alpha \lambda_\alpha^2 \ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha}$, so
\begin{equation}
  S_A = -\sum_{\alpha=1}^{r} \lambda_\alpha^2 \ln \lambda_\alpha^2 \;\le\; \ln r \;\le\; \ln \chi_n,
  \label{eq:arealawbound}
\end{equation}
with equality in the last step exactly when all $\chi_n$ Schmidt weights are equal,
$\lambda_\alpha^2 = 1/\chi_n$. This is the most important equation of this session, and it says
that \emph{the bond dimension sets an upper limit to the entanglement entropy that the ansatz can
represent}. If the state we want has entanglement entropy $S_A$ across some cut, we need at least
$\chi \gtrsim e^{S_A}$ there to describe it. So the question of whether matrix product states are
a good choice for a given problem becomes the question of how $S_A$ behaves as we increase the
system size. Figure~\ref{fig:schmidt-cut} shows what crosses a cut, for two spins, for a whole
chain, for a square lattice and for a random state.

\subsubsection{The area law, and why one dimension is cheap}
The key physical fact is the following. For the ground state of a \emph{gapped}, local,
one-dimensional Hamiltonian, correlations decay exponentially beyond some correlation length
$\xi$, and it turns out that $S_A$ \emph{saturates} to a constant that does not depend on $L$ as
soon as the block $A$ is a few $\xi$ away from both its boundary and the far end of the chain.
This is what is called the area law, and it is proven for gapped one-dimensional systems. Only
the physics within $\sim\xi$ of the cut contributes to the entanglement, and everything deep
inside $A$ or $B$ does not matter for it. For a \emph{gapless} critical chain there is no finite
$\xi$, and conformal field theory tells us that $S_A$ grows instead, but only logarithmically, as
$S_A \sim \tfrac{c}{3}\ln n$ for a cut in the bulk of an infinite or periodic chain. With open
boundaries, which we use in all this session for the reasons given in Section~\ref{sec:bc}, this
is roughly halved to $S_A\sim\tfrac{c}{6}\ln n$. In both cases this is much cheaper than the
generic case. A uniformly random state in the Hilbert space has $S_A$ growing linearly with the
size of the smaller block, which is a \emph{volume law} and is the content of Page's theorem. This
would force $\chi$ to grow exponentially with system size, and the ansatz would lose its purpose.
Ground states of local 1D Hamiltonians live in a very small and special part of the full Hilbert
space, with low or at most logarithmically growing entanglement. It is because they live in this
part that a bond dimension $\chi$ that stays moderate, or grows only polynomially, as
$L\to\infty$ can describe them accurately. Panel (c) of Figure~\ref{fig:schmidt-cut} shows the
three cases next to each other.

A clean way to see the saturation directly is to solve an exactly dimerized chain, where sites
are grouped into decoupled pairs, $\Ham = J\sum_k \vb S_{2k-1}\cdot\vb S_{2k}$ with no coupling
between pairs. The exact ground state is a product of singlets,
$\ket{\Psi} = \bigotimes_k \ket{\Psi_{\mathrm{singlet}}}_{2k-1,2k}$. Now let us cut this state
anywhere. Every cut either falls \emph{inside} a singlet, which contributes $\ln 2$ as follows from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:vonNeumann} above, or falls \emph{between} singlets, which contributes nothing, since
the two halves factorize exactly there. However large we choose the block $A$, as long as its
boundary is between dimers, $S_A$ is either $0$ or $\ln2$ per boundary point, independent of the
size of $A$. This is the area law in its simplest form. Entanglement is a property of the
\emph{boundary} of the cut and not of its volume, and in one dimension a boundary is only a point,
or two points for a block in the interior, so it costs $\Order{1}$ for any system size.

\subsubsection{Why two dimensions is expensive}
The same argument of counting bonds, done in two
dimensions, gives the opposite conclusion. Let us divide a 2D lattice into decoupled clusters, for
example plaquettes of four spins each that are fully coupled inside and disconnected from each
other, and repeat the exercise. A rectangular block $A$ now cuts a number of bonds between
clusters that is proportional to the \emph{perimeter} of $A$, and not a fixed number of points.
Doubling the linear size of $A$ roughly doubles the number of clusters at the boundary, and so it
doubles $S_A$. A matrix product state has to turn the 2D lattice into an effective 1D chain that
snakes through the rows, so the bond in the middle of this snake has to carry the entanglement of
a whole row. As a result, $\chi$ has to grow \emph{exponentially} with the linear dimension of the
system to keep the truncation error fixed. Matrix product states can still be used in two
dimensions, and some of the best available numerics for the 2D Hubbard model and for frustrated
triangular and kagome-lattice antiferromagnets are obtained in this way. However, this only works
on narrow cylinders and with a much larger $\chi$ than a comparable 1D calculation would need. We
will not do two-dimensional calculations in this course, and the goal of the argument is to
explain why everything else in this session is specific to one dimension.

\subsubsection{Where matrix product states struggle even in 1D}
Bounded area-law entanglement is a
property of \emph{ground states} of \emph{gapped or mildly critical, local} Hamiltonians. It
fails, and matrix product states fail with it, in a few situations that we will not compute in
this course but that we should keep in mind. First, entanglement grows \emph{linearly} in time
under quantum quench dynamics, so time evolution with matrix product states can only be trusted up
to some maximal time set by $\chi$. Second, highly excited states deep in the many-body spectrum
typically violate the area law completely. Third, long-range interactions effectively bring back a
higher-dimensional connectivity, even in a chain that is formally 1D.

\subsection{Boundary conditions}
\label{sec:bc}
\subsubsection{Open boundaries and a single cut}
Before we move to the physics, there is one more practical point. The choice between open and
periodic boundary conditions matters a lot for tensor networks, even though it matters very little
for exact diagonalization, where periodic boundary conditions only add one more coupling term to
the Hamiltonian matrix. With open boundary conditions, a cut in the bulk separates exactly
\emph{one} bond of the chain, so the area-law argument above applies directly.

\subsubsection{Periodic boundaries and the cost of two cuts}
With periodic
boundary conditions the chain is a ring, and any bipartition into two contiguous arcs cuts the ring
at \emph{two} points instead of one, since the two arcs are neighbors of each other at both ends.
Both cuts contribute to the entanglement entropy across the partition, so periodic boundary
conditions roughly \emph{double} $S_A$ compared with open boundary conditions on the same chain. By
Eq.~\eqref{eq:arealawbound}, doubling the entropy means that we need roughly the \emph{square} of
the bond dimension to reach the same accuracy. This is why almost every practical matrix product
state calculation, including all the rest of this session, uses open boundary conditions, because
periodic chains are much more expensive for the same physical answer.
Figure~\ref{fig:boundary-cuts} shows the two cases next to each other.

\subsection{Entanglement entropy as a diagnostic for quantum phase transitions}

\subsubsection{The transverse-field Ising model}
\label{sec:tfi}
Entanglement entropy is not only a way to estimate the cost of a tensor network calculation. It is
also a physical diagnostic, and the cleanest case where we can see this is a quantum phase
transition. Let us take the transverse-field Ising chain,
\begin{equation}
  \Ham_{\mathrm{TFI}} = -J\sum_n S_n^z S_{n+1}^z - B_x \sum_n S_n^x.
  \label{eq:tfi}
\end{equation}
This model has two exactly solvable limits. At $B_x=0$, only the $S^zS^z$ term survives, and even
though $S^z_n$ is a quantum operator, the Hamiltonian is diagonal in the $S^z$ basis. The ground
state is then the classical fully polarized ferromagnet $\ket{\up\up\cdots\up}$, or its degenerate
partner $\ket{\dn\dn\cdots\dn}$. At $B_x\to\infty$, we can instead neglect the exchange term
completely, and the ground state is again classical, this time fully polarized along $x$,
$\ket{\to\to\cdots\to}$. Both limits are separable product states, so by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:vonNeumann} their entanglement entropy is exactly zero. Between these limits,
$\Ham_{\mathrm{TFI}}$ must interpolate between two very different classical orders, and it does so
through a quantum phase transition. The exact solution of this model comes from mapping the spins
to free fermions with a Jordan-Wigner transformation, which we will see in detail in Session 5, and
for the normalization used here it places the critical point at $B_x = J/2$. Figure~\ref{fig:tfi}
shows the chain at the two ends of the field and at the transition.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/mps_spin_chains/tfi.pdf}
  \caption{The transverse-field Ising chain of Eq.~\eqref{eq:tfi} at three fields, each cut at
    its central bond. (a) At $B_x=0$ every arrow points along $z$, and the all-down state drawn
    behind it is its degenerate partner. Both are product states, so nothing crosses the
    cut. (b) Near the critical field $B_x=J/2$ the arrows tilt toward $x$ and the chain breaks
    into domains of every size, and here the largest number of strands cross the cut, which is
    the peak of $S_A$ found in the subsection on the entanglement peak and the order parameter.
    (c) For $B_x\gg J$ every arrow points along $x$, which is again a product state with nothing
    across the cut. The point to look at is that both ends are classical and only the middle is
    not.}
  \label{fig:tfi}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{The entanglement peak and the order parameter}
Both classical limits have zero entanglement entropy, while the state between them is quantum,
meaning a superposition that cannot be reduced to a single classical spin configuration. So $S_A$
has to rise from zero somewhere in the middle and go back down, and the natural guess is that it
has its peak at the phase transition. This is what the numerics show. If we compute $S_A$ at the
central bond of a long chain as a function of $B_x/J$, we see that it vanishes at both $B_x=0$ and
$B_x\to\infty$, with a clear maximum at the critical point. For a chain of $\sim 100$ spins, which
is close enough to the thermodynamic limit, this peak is at $B_x/J = 0.5$, the exact value given
above.

We can compare this with a completely independent and more familiar diagnostic, which is the order
parameter $\expval{S_n^z}$. For $B_x < 0.5$, the ground state keeps a finite $\expval{S_n^z}$, the
ferromagnetic order parameter of the Ising phase. For $B_x>0.5$, quantum fluctuations remove it
completely and $\expval{S_n^z}\to 0$, and only $\expval{S_n^x}\ne0$ remains. Both diagnostics agree
on the position of the transition, which is a good sign, but numerically they do not behave
equally well. The order-parameter curve for a finite chain of $20$ spins places the transition
clearly away from $0.5$, and we need to go to $L\sim 100$ before the finite-size estimate
converges to the exact critical value. Exact diagonalization cannot reach the system sizes needed
to resolve this clearly, while matrix product states can.

\subsection{The Heisenberg chain at thermodynamic-limit scale}
We now have the tool, which is matrix product states, and the diagnostics we need to trust it,
which are the energy fluctuation and the convergence in bond dimension. Let us use both on the same
kind of questions that Session 2 asked about the Heisenberg chain, $\Ham_{\mathrm{Heis}} = J\sum_n
\vb S_n\cdot\vb S_{n+1} - B\sum_n S_n^z$, but now at system sizes that were out of reach before.

\subsubsection{Magnetization at zero field and around a magnetic impurity}
At small field $B$, deep inside the gapless phase, the magnetization $\expval{S_n^z}$ of a uniform
Heisenberg chain stays zero at every site. The field is too weak to overcome the antiferromagnetic
exchange, and translational symmetry keeps every site equivalent. This is already a nontrivial
check, since it holds even though we have explicitly broken time-reversal symmetry with $B\ne0$.
The ground state is not yet in the regime where this symmetry breaking appears locally.

The situation changes qualitatively once we replace the uniform field by a single \textbf{magnetic
impurity}, meaning that we keep $B=0$ everywhere except at one site, $n=0$, where we apply a local
field. The system responds with a nonzero magnetization $\expval{S_n^z}$ that varies in space
around the impurity, and \emph{how} this response decays with the distance from the impurity is
one of the clearest diagnostics we have to know if the chain is gapped or gapless. For the uniform
gapless Heisenberg chain, the induced magnetization decays as a slow power law,
\begin{equation}
  \expval{S_n^z} \sim \frac{1}{|n|}, \qquad |n| \gg 1,
  \label{eq:powerlawdecay}
\end{equation}
and it never quite vanishes, however far we go from the impurity. This is the signature of a
gapless system. The chain has excitations at arbitrarily low energy, so a local perturbation can
affect the chain arbitrarily far away with only an algebraic decay.

\subsubsection{The correlation length of a dimerized chain from an impurity response}
Now let us dimerize the exchange coupling, alternating a strong bond $J_1$ and a weak bond $J_2$
along the chain, $\Ham = \sum_n J_n\,\vb S_n\cdot\vb S_{n+1}$ with $J_n$ alternating between $J_1$
and $J_2$. As soon as $J_1\ne J_2$, the chain opens a gap, and if we repeat the impurity
calculation we now find an exponentially decaying response,
\begin{equation}
  \expval{S_n^z} \sim e^{-|n|/\xi},
  \label{eq:expdecay}
\end{equation}
with a correlation length $\xi$ that becomes shorter as the dimerization becomes stronger. This is
a direct numerical demonstration of the physics we expected in Section~\ref{sec:entanglement}. A
finite gap gives a finite correlation length, and the same $\xi$ controls both the exponential
decay of the local response \emph{and} the saturation of the entanglement entropy across any cut.
To obtain a clean exponential fit, which can be clearly distinguished from the power law of the
gapless case, we need chains of order $100$ spins, which is an order of magnitude beyond what
exact diagonalization can reach.

\subsubsection{Excited-state magnetization profiles and the many-body gap}
Beyond the ground state, we can also target a few low excited states directly and look at their
magnetization profiles. In a Heisenberg chain with a small field, the ground state is the usual
$S=0$ singlet with zero magnetization everywhere, but the first excited state has a standing-wave
pattern in $\expval{S_n^z}$, with finite magnetization in some regions of the chain and the
opposite sign in others. The second excited state has again zero magnetization everywhere, since
it has the same time-reversal symmetry as the ground state, while the third excited state has the
same standing wave as the first, related to it by time reversal. This alternation between
symmetric and antisymmetric states is what we saw in the small chains of Session 2, and now we see
that it remains stable at much larger $L$.

Finally, we can follow the many-body gap $E_1 - E_0$ as a function of $L$, for both the uniform and
the dimerized chain. The gap of the uniform Heisenberg chain closes as $L\to\infty$, consistent
with the gapless behavior we found with Eq.~\eqref{eq:powerlawdecay}, while the gap of the
dimerized chain saturates to a finite value, the same value that sets the correlation length $\xi$
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:expdecay}. To see both trends converge clearly, without being cut off by
finite-size effects, we again need the large values of $L$ that only matrix product states can
give us here.

\subsection{Fractionalization in spin-$\tfrac12$ and spin-$1$ chains at large scale}
\label{sec:fractionalization}
We are now able to answer clearly a question that Session 2 could only touch with small chains.
What is the real difference between the spin-$\tfrac12$ and spin-$1$ Heisenberg models?

\subsubsection{Non-local static correlators}
A convenient and purely static probe of the gap is the non-local correlator
$\expval{S_0^z S_n^z}$, which is the linear response at site $n$ to a perturbation applied at site
$0$. We already saw the impurity version of this idea in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:powerlawdecay}
and~\eqref{eq:expdecay}, and the same two behaviors appear directly in the correlator, with a
power-law decay $\sim 1/n$ for a gapless chain and an exponential decay $\sim e^{-n/\xi}$ for a
gapped one. If we apply this to the dimerized model of the previous section with increasing
dimerization strength, we see the trend directly. Stronger dimerization gives a shorter $\xi$ and
a faster exponential decay, while the undimerized limit gives back the slow power law.

Now let us apply the same correlator to the spin-$\tfrac12$ and spin-$1$ Heisenberg models,
\emph{without} any dimerization and only with nearest-neighbor exchange, $\Ham =
J\sum_n \vb S_n\cdot\vb S_{n+1}$ for $S=\tfrac12$ and $S=1$ respectively. Here the system size
matters a lot. If we only had exact diagonalization, which for spin-$1$ is limited to around
$L\sim15$ to $16$, we could think that the spin-$1$ correlator is the \emph{larger} of the two at
those distances. But if we go to $L\sim 40$ to $50$ spins, the picture is completely reversed. The
spin-$\tfrac12$ correlator keeps decaying as a slow power law up to the largest distances, while
the spin-$1$ correlator drops exponentially once $n$ is larger than its correlation length, which
is much shorter. This crossover only becomes visible once the system is comparable to, or larger
than, this correlation length, and this is why small chains give a wrong impression. Spin-$1$ is
gapped and spin-$\tfrac12$ is not, but we need a large enough $L$ to see it.

\subsubsection{Fractionalization in the spin-1 chain}
The contrast is clearest in the following case. Let us take an open spin-$1$ chain and apply a
small uniform field, $B\sim0.05J$. Instead of spreading uniformly, the induced magnetization
$\expval{S_n^z}$ is localized almost completely at the two edges of the chain, and the bulk stays
at essentially zero. Summing the magnetization over each edge region gives $-\tfrac12$ on the left
and $+\tfrac12$ on the right. Each edge behaves as if it had an emergent free spin-$\tfrac12$
degree of freedom, even though every microscopic spin in the chain is spin-$1$. This is what is
called \textbf{fractionalization}, meaning that the elementary excitations of the system are not
integer multiples of the microscopic spin but a real fraction of it. The simplest exactly solvable
model with the same edge physics is the \textbf{AKLT point}. This is a spin-$1$ chain built from
projected valence bonds, which is in the same phase as the Heisenberg chain and in which the
emergent spin-$\tfrac12$ edge states can be computed exactly, and not only observed numerically.
Figure~\ref{fig:aklt} shows how it is built and where the edge spins come from. At $L=50$ the two
edge moments still visibly leak into the bulk, and only at $L=100$ do we see them clearly localized
at the two ends with essentially nothing in between. This confirms that in the thermodynamic limit
the spin-$1$ Heisenberg chain has a fully gapped bulk and a pair of free spin-$\tfrac12$ edge
modes. This is the Haldane phase, which is one of the simplest examples of a symmetry-protected
topological phase in one dimension.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/mps_spin_chains/aklt.pdf}
  \caption{The AKLT construction of the spin-$1$ chain. (a) Each spin-$1$ is written as two
    spin-$\tfrac12$ inside one capsule, which is the projector that keeps only their symmetric
    states with total spin $1$. (b) The right half of each site forms a singlet with the left half
    of the next site, so every bond has one valence bond and the bulk has no free moment. (c) The
    outer halves of the two end sites have no partner, and they are the free spin-$\tfrac12$ at
    the edges. A small field polarizes them, so the two edge regions have moments of
    $-\tfrac12$ and $+\tfrac12$, which is half of what a spin-$1$ can have, while the bulk stays
    at zero.}
  \label{fig:aklt}
\end{figure}

We can see the same physics again, even more directly, in the \textbf{dynamical} correlator
$\expval{S_n^+(t)S_n^-(0)}$ as a function of both position $n$ and energy $\omega$, which is the
same kind of quantity that we used in Session 2 to obtain excitation spectra. In the bulk of the
spin-$1$ chain, creating a spin excitation costs a finite energy, which is the bulk gap we just
discussed. However, at the two edges the dynamical correlator has spectral weight at essentially
\emph{zero} energy, meaning that an edge spin can be flipped at no energy cost. This zero-energy
edge response is the direct spectral signature of the free spin-$\tfrac12$ edge modes. Again, it
only becomes sharp and clear once the chain is long enough, $L\sim 40$ spin-$1$'s or more, so that
the two edges are decoupled from each other and from any remaining contribution of the bulk.

\subsection*{Closing summary}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Matrix product states write the exponentially many wavefunction coefficients
    $C(s_1,\ldots,s_L)$ as a chain of small matrices, Eq.~\eqref{eq:mpsansatz}, and the number of
    parameters grows only linearly in $L$ and quadratically in the bond dimension $\chi$,
    Eq.~\eqref{eq:paramcount}.
  \item This ansatz is only as good as the entanglement of the target state allows. The Schmidt
    decomposition shows that the bond dimension bounds the entanglement entropy across any cut,
    $S_A \le \ln\chi$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:arealawbound}.
  \item Ground states of gapped, local, one-dimensional Hamiltonians obey an area law, meaning that
    their entanglement entropy saturates, or at criticality grows at most logarithmically, instead
    of growing with system size. This is why a moderate $\chi$ that does not depend on $L$ is
    enough in 1D, and why the same ansatz becomes exponentially expensive in two dimensions.
  \item Energy fluctuations, Eq.~\eqref{eq:varH}, allow us to check convergence without knowing
    the exact answer. Open boundary conditions roughly halve the entanglement entropy compared with
    periodic ones, so the required $\chi$ is roughly its square root.
  \item The entanglement entropy is also a physical diagnostic. It has its peak exactly at the
    quantum phase transition of the transverse-field Ising model, which is clearly resolved once
    $L$ is close to the thermodynamic limit.
  \item Repeating the study of the Heisenberg chain of Session 2 at $40$ to $100$ spins and more
    turns qualitative hints into sharp and converged results. We find power-law versus
    exponential correlators, a many-body gap that closes versus one that saturates, and a clear
    separation between the gapless spin-$\tfrac12$ chain and the gapped, fractionalized spin-$1$
    chain with its emergent spin-$\tfrac12$ edge modes.
\end{itemize}

Matrix product states solved our $2^L$ problem for spin chains, but everything we did here was
written in the spin language of Session 2. In the next session we go back to the fermions of
Session 3 and connect them to spins through the Jordan-Wigner mapping, which we only mentioned in
Section~\ref{sec:tfi}. We will also present the general tensor-network algorithm, which is the
variational sweep that produces the matrices $M_n^{s_n}$ that we have used in this session without
showing how to obtain them.
