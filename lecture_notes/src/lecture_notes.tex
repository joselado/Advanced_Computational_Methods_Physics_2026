\section*{How this course is organized}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{\textbf{How this course is organized}}

Every session of this course has five pieces, and we should use them together, not one instead
of another. The recording is the lecture itself, with the derivations done live and with the
questions that came up during the lecture. The slides show the definitions, figures and equations
in the order of the lecture, so they are the fastest way to find a point in a recording or to
review a session before the exam. These lecture notes follow the same sessions in the same order.
The difference is that they write the physics as a connected text, and they do step by step what a
slide does in one line. So we can read them instead of watching a session a second time, or next
to the slides, to fill in what a bullet point skips.

The Jupyter notebook of each session is named in the box under the session title, and the
\emph{Computational companion} boxes inside the text point to it again. The notebooks are the place
where the physics is computed. Nothing there is a fixed figure, and you are meant to run the cells,
change the parameters and see what happens. Finally, every session has four exercise shorts, which
are short silent films of one to two minutes each, and they are at
\url{https://joselado.github.io/Advanced_Computational_Methods_Physics_2026/exercise_shorts/}. Each
film shows one idea of the session, and every number on the screen is computed with the same codes
as the notebooks.

None of these pieces is complete alone. The recordings and the slides give the story and the
intuition, the notes give derivations that we can check line by line, and the notebooks give the
numerics, which build a kind of intuition that the other pieces cannot give. A good way to work on
a session is to watch the recording once, and then read the notes with the notebook open next to
them. After this, we go back to the notebook and change things, until the results feel like our own
calculation and not like something that happened on the screen of someone else.

\subsection*{The computational libraries}

All the numerics of the course are done with two Python libraries, and not with code written from
zero inside the notebooks. Both libraries have to be installed before the notebooks can run, and
the first cell of each notebook says which environment variables they need.

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{\texttt{pyqula}}, at \url{https://github.com/joselado/pyqula}, does single-particle
    and mean-field electronic structure. This means tight-binding Hamiltonians, band structures,
    and the self-consistent Hubbard and charge-density-wave calculations of Session 1. The notebook
    of Session 1, \texttt{classical\_magnetism.ipynb}, uses it.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{dmrgpy}}, at \url{https://github.com/joselado/dmrgpy}, does the quantum
    many-body calculations. These are the exact diagonalization of spin and fermionic Hamiltonians,
    and the matrix-product-state and DMRG calculations of the later sessions. The notebooks of
    Sessions 2 to 5 use it.
\end{itemize}

We use both libraries as tools. To follow the course you do not need to read their source code,
and it is enough to call the functions that the notebooks show.
